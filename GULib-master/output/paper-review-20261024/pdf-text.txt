Auditing Strategic Deletion Requests in Graph Unlearning
Anonymous Author(s)
Teaser figure placeholder
Figure 1: Placeholder for the audit overview and core response-pattern finding.
Abstract
Graph unlearning (GU) methods are being investigated as a family
of machine unlearning techniques for protecting data privacy. These
methods face distinct challenges due to interdependencies among
nodes and the underlying graph topology. However, red-teaming
of GU methods through strategically selected deletion requests re-
mains underexplored. More specifically, the different components
of GU performance changes under such attacks have received lim-
ited analysis. To address these gaps, we propose a framework from
an adversarial perspective. It uses deletion-target selectors to con-
struct adversarial requests and examines how GU methods respond
under these attacks. Within this framework, we define attacker
access levels, including gray-box and white-box settings, based on
the information available for deletion-target selection. Our exten-
sive experiments demonstrate how performance decomposition
and complementary metrics distinguish GU responses to deletion
requests. Our case studies reveal method- and dataset-dependent
responses, with substantial utility degradation occurring even un-
der random deletion and targeted requests producing divergent
effects on GU and full retraining. The framework facilitates selector
design and informs the development of more robust GU methods.
Keywords
graph unlearning, adversarial deletion requests, robustness, graph
neural networks

1 Introduction
Graph unlearning (GU) studies how a trained graph model should
respond when selected nodes, edges, or features must be removed.
WWW ’27, Dublin, Ireland
2027.
The problem is relevant to data-erasure requests, but graph struc-
ture makes the update different from removing an isolated training
example: message passing couples a node’s representation and
prediction to its neighborhood [6, 12, 19]. A deletion request can
therefore change both the status of the requested records and the
graph context used by the remaining model.
Existing GU methods reduce the cost of retraining by using
partition-based updates, influence estimates, or learned deletion-
aware procedures [1, 3, 5, 15, 21]. These methods provide different
update mechanisms and need not produce the same post-deletion
model as full retraining. Consequently, a lower score after a request
does not by itself identify what changed: it may reflect the method’s
pre-deletion reference, the effect of deleting those records even
under retraining, or an additional difference between the GU output
and the retrained output.
Deletion requests also expose a choice to the requester. If an
interface accepts a set of eligible records, a requester may choose
that set strategically instead of sampling it benignly. This paper
studies that request-selection step. The attacker selects a deletion set
subject to an explicit candidate pool, information access, and budget;
the attacker does not control model training or the implementation
of the victim GU method. We use the term attack to describe this
constrained request construction, not to imply that every selected
request harms every method.
Figure 1 motivates a second dimension beyond the size of a
utility change: the response pattern across requests. In the illus-
trated PubMed setting, the same four request types induce different
mean groupings across GU methods and full retraining. This moti-
vates comparing both performance levels and request-dependent
responses, while retaining paired uncertainty and dataset-specific
scope.
Our evaluation asks two related questions: whether selection
changes the requests presented to a GU system, and how the re-
sulting GU behavior compares with both a budget-matched ran-
dom request and full retraining on the same request. We separate

2 Related Work
Graph unlearning.Graph-unlearning methods include approaches
based on partitioning and partial retraining, influence-function
updates, and learned deletion-aware objectives. GraphEraser and
GraphRevoker are partition-based approaches [ 1, 22]; GIF esti-
mates deletion effects with influence functions [21]; GNNDelete
and MEGU use learned update procedures [3, 15]; and IDEA studies
certified graph unlearning [5]. These methods motivate evaluation
across update mechanisms. Our question is different from proposing
another update rule: we study how the choice of deletion request
interacts with the output of an existing GU method.
Security and auditing of machine unlearning.Prior work has ex-
amined poisoning or adversarial behavior around machine-unlearning
procedures, as well as the reliability of empirical unlearning evalu-
ation [4, 9, 16, 23]. Privacy attacks on graph models provide related
tools for studying information exposed by graph predictions [8, 17].
Our setting focuses on a distinct control point: the requester chooses
which eligible graph elements to submit for deletion. The result-
ing utility difference is an audit outcome, not by itself a privacy
guarantee or proof of forgetting.
Influence and target selection.Influence maximization selects
sets to increase expected propagation or coverage in a graph [ 7,
11, 14]. Influence functions and gradient-tracing methods estimate
how training examples affect model objectives or parameters [13,
18]. These signals provide possible selection objectives, but their
scores are not definitions of downstream GU error. We therefore
evaluate the selected request after the GU and retraining procedures,
rather than treating selector scores or selected-set overlap as attack
effectiveness.
Positioning.This work connects request-selection mechanisms
to a paired audit of GU outcomes. It distinguishes selector objective,
request identity, post-deletion utility, and the full-retraining refer-
ence. The evaluation is intended to explain the conditions under
which the observed responses differ; it does not assume that the
methods form a universal vulnerability ordering.

5 Experimental Design
We organize the experiments around four questions:
(1) Do the selection mechanisms produce distinct deletion sets
under matched candidates and budgets?
(2) Do the selectors’ reference or proxy objectives align with
their stated targets?
(3) Relative to a budget-matched random request, how does a
selected request change the measured GU outcome?
(4) How do these responses vary across GU methods, datasets,
and attacker access settings, and how large is the signed
gap from same-request retraining?
5.1 Ten-seed illustrative response-pattern case
Figure 1 uses a separate, later experiment batch: PubMed, split seed
2024, ten training seeds (42, 212, 2024, and 0–6), and deletion of
10% of training-node candidates. Random, Degree, R-point, and
D-full share the candidate pool and budget; the IF requests use a
GCN selection source. Deletion removes training supervision and
incident edges while retaining isolated feature rows; evaluation
uses the original graph. Random’s three request draws are averaged
within each training seed. Each GU endpoint is matched to full-
graph GCN retraining on the same request. GraphEraser is a shard
ensemble, so this reference does not isolate its architecture-specific
approximation error. The metric is test accuracy. We show seed-
level means and pointwise 95% 𝑡 intervals; the illustrative cases
are exploratory, not simultaneous tests of a complete ranking. The
three-seed matrix below is a distinct historical batch and is not
pooled with this figure.
5.2 Primary multi-method matrix
The primary matrix covers node-deletion requests on Cora, Cite-
Seer, and PubMed with an OpenGU GCN backbone. We compare
six graph-unlearning methods: GIF, GNNDelete, MEGU, IDEA, Gra-
phEraser, and GraphRevoker [1, 3, 5, 15, 21]. Each request removes
10% of the eligible training-node candidates. The five selectors are
Random, Degree, PageRank, R-point, and D-full. R-point is the
practical influence-based setting used in the main-text comparison;
D-full is a last-layer, two-hop setting included for reference.
The matrix contains 315 planned method conditions: 270 GU out-
puts and 45 full-retraining references. All 270 GU outputs have an
identity-matched retraining result. We report three training seeds
(42, 212, and 2024) as mean and sample standard deviation. In this
matrix, Random, Degree, and PageRank reuse a selection across
training seeds, whereas R-point and D-full selections vary with
training seed. Thus, the reported spread includes model-training
variability and, for the influence-based selectors, selection variabil-
ity; it is not an estimate over independent random requests.

5.4 Metrics and aggregation
The primary matrix reports the F1 values recorded in its analysis
report. We aggregate at the training-seed level and retain signed
paired differences. For AAGU-032/053, the serialized utility field is
named f1, but the output code computes test accuracy by comparing
predicted and true labels on the test mask. We call that endpoint
test accuracy throughout and keep it separate from the primary F1
tables.
For the AAGU-053 comparison, each dataset-level mean gives
equal weight to its 16 matched requests. For the AAGU-032 selector
comparison, we first average over three training seeds within each
configuration and budget, then summarize the 12 configuration-
budget groups per dataset. Those groups share configurations and
are not independent samples. We use these results descriptively
and do not report significance or equivalence from them.

GIF and IDEA have the same reported value across these three
selectors in this case. These entries show why post-request utility
and a retraining reference answer different questions. They do not
establish a universal ordering of selectors or methods.
The full five-selector tables in Appendix C preserve additional
conditions and both reported outcomes. CiteSeer and PubMed do
not reproduce one common ordering. For example, the GNNDelete
gap is slightly negative under Random on CiteSeer (−0.30pp), but
positive under Random on PubMed (+4.24pp); its R-point gap also
changes from +0.40pp on CiteSeer to −1.56pp on PubMed. These
are descriptive three-seed means, not evidence of equivalence when
a mean is near zero.
6.2 Retraining and GU references expose
different sources of change
In the separate AAGU-032 analysis, the 3000-epoch D-full configura-
tions have lower test accuracy than Degree in 8 of 12 configuration-
budget groups on Cora and 10 of 12 on PubMed, but only 4 of 12
on CiteSeer. The mean paired differences (D-full minus Degree)
are −0.277, +0.188, and −0.922pp for Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed,
respectively. This is evidence about the retraining endpoint alone;
it cannot be extended to a GU method.
For AAGU-053, Table 3 decomposes the same-request compari-
son between the pre-request GNNDelete reference, full retraining,
and GNNDelete. Cora and PubMed have positive mean retraining
gaps, whereas CiteSeer is near zero. The decomposition preserves
the sign and reports distinct descriptive terms by dataset. A near-
zero dataset average does not imply request-level equivalence.
The current AAGU-053 update-detection AUC is 0.587, 0.731,
and 0.509 for Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed, respectively. It uses
deleted nodes as positives, test nodes as negatives, and prediction-
probability change as the score. We report it only as the imple-
mented update-detection diagnostic; it is not a general membership-
inference or privacy guarantee.
7 Discussion
The cases illustrate the value of reporting more than a post-deletion
score. The Cora matrix shows substantial gaps between some GU
outputs and full retraining, while the matched analysis shows that
the size and sign of those gaps depend on both method and dataset.
A single average score would hide whether a change comes from
the request’s effect under retraining or from the additional GU–
retraining difference. The decompositions make these reference
questions visible; they do not identify a causal mechanism by them-
selves.
The results also caution against treating a selector’s score as a
direct measure of attack effectiveness. R-point, D-full, and structural
selectors optimize or rank requests using different information.
Their downstream utility outcomes can vary by dataset and GU
method. The current results do not establish that influence and
structural objectives are misaligned as a general rule, nor do they
show that one objective is universally preferable.
For future GU evaluations, a useful protocol should disclose
the eligible request pool and attacker information, report multiple
independent random requests, and pair each selected request with
both its GU result and a full-retraining reference when feasible. It
should keep test utility, prediction disagreement, update detection,
and forgetting or privacy claims separate. These measurements
support a more interpretable audit while preserving the limits of
each endpoint.
The method-specific case studies support an audit perspective
rather than a benchmark ranking. They do not imply that every ap-
proximate GU method is unsafe, that lower test utility means better
forgetting, or that a result on citation graphs transfers unchanged
to other graph tasks.

9 Conclusion
We presented an audit framework for studying how strategically
selected deletion requests interact with graph-unlearning methods.
It separates request selection from the victim update procedure
and distinguishes post-request utility, budget-matched random re-
sponse, and the signed difference from full retraining on the same
request. The current case studies show that these reference com-
parisons expose method- and dataset-dependent behavior that a
single post-deletion score would not describe.
The evidence in this draft is provisional and supports scoped ob-
servations rather than a universal selector or vulnerability ranking.
A complete evaluation should pair utility with request-level, predic-
tion, and privacy diagnostics, while stating the attacker information
and the limits of each metric.

Interpretation and validation. 𝐺𝑀 is a signed utility difference,
rather than a distance between predictive distributions. Retrain-
ing is a reference, not a test-utility upper bound. A negative gap
therefore remains meaningful and is not clipped or replaced by its
absolute value. For methods with their own architecture or ensem-
ble, the gap can contain differences already present before deletion;
it is not automatically a pure update-approximation error. Subtract-
ing the Random gap measures how that relative deficit changes
with request selection, without establishing a causal mechanism
for the change.
The decompositions preserve observed endpoint differences.
Their diagnostic value is assessed through comparisons showing
which components vary across methods, selectors, budgets, or ac-
cess conditions, and whether those components distinguish cases
with similar aggregate utility changes. Algebraic closure alone does
not establish such empirical value. Performance agreement and gap
reduction also do not independently establish that deleted informa-
tion has been forgotten.

B.3 Update-detection diagnostic
The matched GNNDelete analysis uses deleted nodes as positives
and test nodes as negatives, with the ℓ2 change in predicted class
probabilities between the pre-request and post-request model as
its score. The resulting ROC AUC measures how well these two
groups are distinguished by the observed update. It is reported as an
update-detection diagnostic. A membership-inference or forgetting
interpretation would require its own attacker definition, sampling
controls, and validation; this diagnostic is not used as a general
privacy guarantee.
